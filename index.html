How I Faced My Fear of Arduino—and What Happened Next

Starting something you know nothing about can feel scary, especially when everyone around you seems ahead. But sometimes, one small step—even a shaky one—can change how you see yourself. 🙂

I’m now a second-year student at NU, but back in senior high, I was a TVL-ICT student who focused only on web development. Because of that, I barely knew anything about embedded systems. The idea of working with wires, sensors, resistors, and boards honestly scared me. Coding was fine—but coding with hardware felt like a whole different world.

My friends already had experiences with Arduino. They talked about their old projects, shared stories about how they built things, and I remember thinking, “Wow, they’re so smart. Will I ever understand that?” Their confidence made me feel overwhelmed, like I was already behind before the class even started.

Then came second year—and our Embedded Systems course. We had to use the Arduino UNO for the whole semester. I felt that mix of fear and pressure, but also a quiet thought: If my friends learned it, maybe I can too.

So I pushed myself.

One night, I studied for almost five hours straight, just trying to understand how to light a single LED. That tiny bulb tested my patience. I searched about polarity, what a resistor actually does, and why certain wires must be placed in certain pins. Without a mentor, every step felt like guessing—but I kept trying.

I used Tinkercad too, a simulator where we could practice Arduino virtually. It wasn’t easy at first, but slowly I learned both the software and hardware sides. Every error taught me something.

Then the day of our group activity arrived.

Instead of being nervous, I felt excited. After all the self-study I did, I wanted to share what I learned with my teammates. So I explained the basics, showed them how each part worked, and helped them see the logic behind both the real setup and the Tinkercad version.

We were stressed, though—our laptop battery was at 18%, and my groupmate forgot the charger. We had to finish before it died. While our professor was still explaining, I quickly sketched a clean blueprint for our wiring. I wanted to avoid messy cables because too many wires make LEDs hard to see and everything looks chaotic.

As soon as we ran to our table, we split roles:

  • two members handled the Tinkercad prototype
  • one worked on the code
  • and I focused on the physical wiring

The moment everything finally lit up—both the virtual and physical versions—we knew we did it. And surprisingly, we were the first group to finish.

That day reminded me that fear doesn’t mean we’re incapable—it just means we’re about to learn something new. I realized that starting from zero is not a weakness. Sometimes it becomes the reason we work harder, stay curious, and grow beyond what we expected.

If there’s something you’re scared to try, maybe it’s the thing that will show you how far you can go. Keep learning at your own pace—and trust that every small step still counts.
